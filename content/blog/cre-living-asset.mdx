In commercial real estate, a vertical garden is rarely just a garden. It is part of the property's argument for why tenants should choose that building, stay longer, and believe the operator is investing in the space.

That is why the buying frame matters. If the installation is treated as a decorative upgrade, it will be judged by first impressions. If it is treated as a living asset, it has to be evaluated by performance, serviceability, reporting, and tenant use.

## The buyer is fighting sameness

Many office, mixed-use, hospitality, and premium residential properties compete on similar claims: better amenities, better wellness, better sustainability, better experience. A managed vertical garden can make those claims visible.

The value is not only biophilic design. It is the combination of nature, production, service, and measurable story. A lobby that grows herbs for a cafe or microgreens for a tenant event communicates something different from a static wall finish.

## ESG needs evidence

Sustainability language is easy to overstate. A serious vertical garden program should be careful about proof.

Useful reporting might include water use assumptions, harvest volume, crop type, food miles avoided, service cadence, waste handling, and tenant engagement. Some metrics will require project-specific measurement. If those numbers are not available, the copy should stay qualitative until the program produces real evidence.

## Service protects the brand

A poorly maintained living feature can damage trust faster than no feature at all. CRE teams should ask practical questions before procurement:

- Who services the garden when tenants are present?
- What happens when a crop fails?
- How are pests, sanitation, and plant replacement handled?
- Who owns signage, data, and claims?
- How does the farm change when the tenant mix changes?

The maintenance plan is not a back-office detail. It is part of the tenant experience.

## Next action

Before pricing a vertical garden, write the asset thesis in one paragraph. Name the space, the audience, the business goal, the reporting need, and the service expectation. If the thesis only says "make the lobby greener," the project needs sharper commercial intent.
